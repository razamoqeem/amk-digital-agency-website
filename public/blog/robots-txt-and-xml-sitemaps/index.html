robots.txt and XML sitemaps: a beginner's guide

robots.txt tells crawlers where they may go; a sitemap tells them which pages exist. Here is how each works and the limits Google applies.

By KivoReach · Published 18 November 2026 · 2 min read · 339 words

On this page
  1. What robots.txt does
  2. What an XML sitemap does
  3. Quick check for your site

Two small files help search engines find their way around a website. robots.txt sets rules about where crawlers may go. An XML sitemap lists the pages you want them to know about.

What robots.txt does

The robots.txt file lives at the top level of a host, for example https://example.com/robots.txt. It tells crawlers which parts of the site they may request. Google ignores anything in the file after its first 500 KiB.

robots.txt does not hide pages

A page blocked in robots.txt can still appear in search results, without a description, if other pages link to it. To keep a page out of results, use a noindex rule instead, and do not block that page in robots.txt, or crawlers will never see the noindex.

What an XML sitemap does

A sitemap is a list of the addresses you want search engines to discover, with optional details such as when each page last changed.

Sitemap facts from Google's documentation
ItemWhat Google says
Size of one sitemapUp to 50,000 addresses or 50 MB uncompressed
Larger sitesSplit into several sitemaps, optionally listed in a sitemap index file
<lastmod>Used when it is consistently and verifiably accurate
<priority> and <changefreq>Ignored by Google

Quick check for your site

  • robots.txt is at the top level of the host
  • It does not block pages you want in search results
  • Pages you want hidden use noindex and are not blocked
  • Your sitemap lists only real, working pages
  • The sitemap's address is listed in robots.txt or submitted in Search Console

Our free Robots.txt and Sitemap Checker reads a site's crawl rules, finds its sitemaps and counts the addresses they list.

Do I need a sitemap for a small website?

Not always. If every page is well linked from your other pages, search engines can usually find it. A sitemap still helps them discover new and updated pages, and it is simple to add.

References

  1. Introduction to robots.txt — Google Search Central
  2. How Google interprets the robots.txt specification — Google Search Central
  3. Build and submit a sitemap — Google Search Central

Key takeaways

  • robots.txt manages crawling, not indexing: a blocked page can still appear in results without a description.
  • To keep a page out of results, use noindex, and let crawlers reach the page so they can see it.
  • One sitemap holds up to 50,000 addresses or 50 MB uncompressed; split larger sets.
